# AI as an Equaliser: How Technology Can Dismantle Barriers to Entry for Underrepresented Lawyers and Founders

*A Joint Whitepaper by Hemisphere Consultants and Knowledge Nexus*

---

**Executive Summary**

Artificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping industries at unprecedented speed, creating efficiencies, unlocking new opportunities, and democratising knowledge. Yet its benefits are not distributed equally. For many women, minority professionals, and underrepresented founders, systemic barriers continue to restrict access to both AI tools and the ecosystems in which they thrive.

This whitepaper explores AI’s role as an equaliser in the legal and entrepreneurial arenas. It outlines how technology can help dismantle barriers to entry for underrepresented lawyers and founders, while also addressing the AI gender gap the structural imbalance that risks turning AI into another gatekeeper rather than a gateway.

---

**AI at a Crossroads**

AI is no longer on the horizon; it is here, embedded in daily workflows. From contract drafting to pitch deck development, its applications are vast. Yet, access remains stratified.

Lawyers from underrepresented groups often lack the networks, mentorship, and financial backing to compete with peers who benefit from established privilege.

Founders from diverse backgrounds face persistent challenges in accessing funding, markets, and visibility.

In two decades working across legal tech, strategy, and operations, Knowledge Nexus has seen first-hand that technology doesn’t transform industries, people do. And right now, the people driving AI adoption often reflect the same structural inequities that have long existed in law and entrepreneurship.

Lawyers from underrepresented groups still face barriers in accessing the networks, mentorship, and investment needed to meaningfully leverage these tools. Similarly, founders from diverse or non-traditional backgrounds continue to face disproportionate challenges in accessing funding, markets, and visibility. And at the level of the tools they build and deploy, studies of generative AI models show that women are under-represented in AI-generated imagery of professional roles, and algorithmic systems still embed gendered patterns (smiling women, male default for leadership roles).

If designed and deployed inclusively, AI has the power to change that. It can open doors, level access to knowledge, and amplify underrepresented voices, but only if we consciously build for it.

Recent research underscores both the pace and polarity of adoption. According to [LexisNexis](https://www.linkedin.com/company/lexisnexis/)’ AI Culture Clash report (Aug 2025), 61% of UK legal professionals now use generative AI for work, up from 46% just months earlier. The IDC and Relativity Generative AI in Legal study (Nov 2024) found that 48% of all AI use in legal is now generative, and 69% of professionals expect their usage to increase further within two years. Yet, only 17% of firms say AI is fully embedded in their strategy, suggesting enthusiasm without equal enablement.

The gap between experimentation and transformation is still a wide one. The challenge ahead isn’t just about deploying the next tool, it’s about ensuring that adoption is equitable, ethical, and effective.

If we look at the growing list of AI use cases in legal practice (see below) - these are only some of the main ones - it’s clear that this is an area that will only continue to expand in scale and sophistication.

Contract review & clause flagging Document review & due diligence eDiscovery & litigation support Legal research & summarisation Compliance & regulatory monitoring Legal operations & spend analytics Knowledge management & precedent retrieval Client-facing drafting support (pitch decks, proposals, client updates) Litigation analytics & case strategy Workflow & process automation

AI sits at a crossroads; it can either reinforce existing privilege and bias or it can redefine opportunity. The difference lies in how we lead, educate, and implement, and whether we make inclusivity part of the innovation process, not an afterthought.

**AI as an Equaliser**

**Levelling Knowledge Access**

Where once legal research or market intelligence required significant financial investment, AI-powered tools now deliver information at speed and scale. This enables underrepresented lawyers and founders to compete without prohibitive costs. Previously teams without deep networks and expensive subscriptions were forced to compete on less than equal footing. AI changes that dynamic.

These tools reduce cost and time barriers; tasks that once took hours (or days) can now be delivered in minutes. They open access to insights that were previously locked behind high cost or institutional ability. They bring scale to non-traditional practitioners: solo lawyers, diverse-founder start-ups, legal-ops teams outside major firms. They also hold enormous potential to improve access to justice. Take, for example, the opioid litigation cases in the US, where claimants were once buried under truckloads of discovery documents, tactics designed to exhaust smaller legal teams and inflate costs. Today, AI-powered review and eDiscovery tools could have surfaced critical evidence in hours, not months, dramatically reducing the resource gap between large corporate defendants and those seeking accountability.

---

**Automating Routine Work**

By automating administrative or repetitive tasks, AI frees up capacity for underrepresented professionals to focus on strategic growth, client relationships, and advocacy.

In every transformation project, from legal ops implementations to firm wide process reviews, one thing has always stood out: lawyers and legal professionals spend an extraordinary amount of time on low-value, manual work.

According to [Thomson Reuters](https://www.linkedin.com/company/thomson-reuters/) ’ *State of the Legal Market 2025* report, up to 40% of a lawyer’s time is consumed by routine administrative tasks such as document drafting, matter intake, and reporting. ([legal.thomsonreuters.com](http://legal.thomsonreuters.com/))

AI is rapidly shifting that balance. Automation tools can now generate first-draft contracts, summarise depositions, flag anomalies in discovery sets, and auto-populate matter trackers, saving both time and cost. A 2024 Law Society survey found that 63% of firms adopting AI reported measurable efficiency gains, and 52% said they had already redirected that time into higher-value strategic work. ([lawsociety.org.uk](http://lawsociety.org.uk/))

For underrepresented lawyers and smaller practices, this is not just about efficiency, it’s about equity. Automation narrows the operational gap between large firms with dedicated support teams and solo or small practitioners managing everything themselves. By shifting administrative burdens to technology, smaller players can compete on expertise and insight rather than scale of infrastructure.

---

**Expanding Mentorship & Networks**

AI can simulate aspects of mentorship, providing personalised guidance, curated learning paths, and even access to virtual communities. For those historically excluded from traditional networks, this represents a powerful levelling up mechanism.

**The AI Gender Gap**

Despite its promise, AI reflects the biases of those who build it. According to UNESCO, only 22% of AI professionals worldwide are women. This imbalance has real-world consequences:

Algorithms risk perpetuating existing discrimination.

Funding and hiring recommendations powered by biased data could systematically disadvantage women and minorities.

The absence of diverse perspectives in development stifles innovation and reinforces exclusion. Closing the AI gender gap is not just a moral imperative it is a business and societal one.

The [OpenAI](https://www.linkedin.com/company/openai/) 2025 usage report, however, signals a positive shift. The gender gap in AI adoption among users of ChatGPT has been narrowing rapidly. Women now represent a far greater proportion of professional users than in previous years, a trend attributed to growing awareness, better accessibility, and the integration of AI into mainstream professional workflows. ([cdn.openai.com](http://cdn.openai.com/)).

Yet, the structural barriers remain. Studies show that women are 63% less likely to secure venture funding for AI or tech driven startups compared to men ([oecd.org](http://oecd.org/)), and when they do, they often receive smaller investments despite equivalent performance indicators. In AI academia, women make up only 14% of authorship on leading machine learning papers ([next100years.org.uk](http://next100years.org.uk/)).

Beyond equity, the cost of imbalance is innovation itself. Closing the AI gender gap is therefore not only a moral imperative, but also a business and societal necessity.

Empowering more women to adopt, shape, and lead in AI will determine whether this technology becomes a force for fairness, or a mirror of our existing inequities.

---

**Pathways Forward**

For AI to serve as a true equaliser, action must be deliberate:

Inclusive Design: ensuring diversity is embedded in AI development, from datasets to decision-making frameworks. Accessible Training: Making AI literacy affordable and relevant for underrepresented lawyers and founders. Cross-Sector Collaboration: Leveraging the strengths of consultancies, law firms, tech hubs, and academia to create equitable adoption pathways. Ethical Governance: Advocating for policies that mandate transparency, fairness, and accountability in AI.

**Wrap up**

AI has the potential to be a leveller, dismantling barriers that have historically kept women and underrepresented groups at the margins of law and entrepreneurship. But this outcome is not guaranteed.

If inclusivity, accessibility, and ethical design remain central, AI can shift from a tool of privilege to a platform of possibility, empowering underrepresented lawyers and founders not just to participate, but to lead.
