# Legal Tech Adoption: Why Culture Eats Strategy (and AI) for Breakfast

If you’ve followed me on here, you’ll know I spend most of my time talking about AI & tech in legal, the hype, the risks, the opportunities, and the lessons I’ve learnt over 20 years working with clients.

But here’s the part nobody likes to say out loud: **no technology works if nobody uses it.**

I’ve lost count of how many times I’ve sat with legal teams, both in-house and private practice, and heard some version of: *“Oh yeah, we’ve got loads of tools, or we’ve got X tool (I'm not going to name and shame the tool sorry guys* 👀)*… but no one actually uses it.”*

Lets take an in-house team I spoke to recently who invested in a workflow tool designed to give the GC oversight of team activity. Not to play “big brother,” but to generate insights they could share with stakeholders and the board.

On paper, it looked great. In reality? **Failure**

Why? Because the people and processes were left out of the buying decision.

The tool didn’t integrate with the systems lawyers actually use day-to-day: Outlook, Word, SharePoint. To the team, it was just another layer of admin… to *reduce admin.* You can guess the result: nobody used it, and the system that promised so much delivered nothing.

A strong Legal Ops team (or consultant) would have spotted this. This is part of the work I do daily, asking the questions lawyers don’t always think of, mapping the bottlenecks, and finding where real value comes from automating tasks. Notice what’s missing here: *tech.* Because the hard truth is, this was never a technology problem. It was a people and process problem.

👉 Lesson learned: **never buy tech until you truly understand how your people work.**

You can invest in the most advanced CLM, the slickest matter management system, or the smartest AI contract review tool. If lawyers aren’t logging in, if processes aren’t embedded, if the business doesn’t trust the outputs, you’ve just bought yourself very expensive shelfware.

And here’s the real kicker: **adoption isn’t about technology. It’s about people, trust, and culture.**

---

#### The Missing Piece: A Dedicated Legal Ops Role

In my last article, I talked about Legal Ops being the backbone of legal transformation. What I didn’t emphasise enough is this: you need someone with the time and capacity to make it all stick.

Too often, legal ops responsibilities are handed to already overstretched in-house lawyers “on the side of the desk.” The reality? Change initiatives stall, tools gather dust, and nobody owns the roadmap.

A dedicated Legal Ops role (or team) is not a “nice to have.” It’s the right hand of the CLO/GC. The function that:

**Aligns** the legal roadmap with wider business goals.**Translates** business priorities into legal initiatives.**Frees up** leadership to focus on lawyering and strategy.**Builds** the foundations for AI readiness and manages ongoing AI governance.

Think of Legal Ops as the strategic integrato**r** : making sure legal isn’t just supporting the business today but also anticipating where it’s heading tomorrow.

Without this, technology adoption is left to chance, and chance rarely delivers.

Lets put this into perspective. A GC once told me they wanted a contract tool. But here’s the thing, selling that internally as *“legal needs it”* or *“legal has a bottleneck”* rarely lands with a CEO or CFO. What lands with them are business challenges.

So I asked them: *why do you really need this?* And no, I didn’t want to hear about features or a tool that does x and y. I want to know what problem we are trying to solve.

Because here’s the truth no vendor is telling you...

Contract tech is usually the first thing in-house teams look at, but it’s also one of the vaguest. There are so many different angles:

Are you automating NDAs or simple contracts? Are you handling complex agreements? Or, like many businesses, are you actually dealing with mostly third-party paper, in which case, all that time spent automating your own templates - complete waste of time.

In this case, the business (a large global corporate) had a real issue: the materials needed to make their product didn’t arrive. Why? Because a supplier contract expired without anyone knowing. The contract had been managed by the business, not legal, and by the time legal were looped in it was already the eleventh hour.

Enter frantic negotiations. Because renewing a supplier agreement isn’t just about cost. It’s about:

**Compliance** with regulations, sanctions, and sector-specific requirements.**ESG obligations** , making sure the supplier still aligns with the company’s sustainability commitments.**What’s changed with the supplier** , financial health, ownership, operational capacity, or even geopolitical exposure.**Quality and delivery standards** , ensuring the goods meet the company’s requirements and timelines (missed deliveries can shut down production lines).**Pricing and indexation clauses** , particularly with inflation or currency fluctuations.**Termination and exit rights** , what happens if the supplier can’t perform or the company needs to pivot quickly.**Liability and indemnities** , especially around defective goods, IP, or product safety.**Data and confidentiality** , if the supplier touches sensitive information or has access to systems.**Risk allocation and insurance coverage** , to protect the business if things go wrong.

All of these are business-critical. Yet to the wider organisation, it often looks like legal is “delaying things” when in reality they’re protecting the company from supply chain disruption, reputational risk, and financial loss.”

But here’s the kicker: this wasn’t a problem that needed the shiny contract AI everyone is telling you about. It actually needed something far simpler, a robust contract management tool. One that allows business and legal to interact, that gives legal oversight, and that sets standards. Imagine a system that simply alerts you when a supplier contract is due to expire in 3–6 months. That alone would have avoided the crisis.

Now the GC has a story they can take to the CFO. Not “we need a tool because legal is drowning,” but: *“We need a tool to protect the company’s bottom line, to keep products moving, orders fulfilled, and revenue flowing.”*

And that’s the point: it’s no longer a legal problem. It’s a business problem. And that’s the language that gets buy-in.

#### Adoption Starts with People, Not Tech

Let me be blunt: the biggest barrier to adoption isn’t the tool. It’s the people.

Lawyers are trained to spot risks, not embrace change. That mindset means tech adoption will fail if it’s imposed rather than co-created. Legal Ops has to flip the script by focusing on three pillars:

#### 1. Culture & Trust

**Involvement builds trust.** If lawyers feel a system was “done to them” instead of “built with them,” adoption rates plummet.**Transparency matters.** Be honest about why changes are happening. Is it cost, efficiency, client service? The “why” is more important than the “what.”**Quick wins earn credibility.** Show people small, tangible benefits early, and you build momentum for bigger change.

#### 2. Training & Enablement

One lunch-and-learn won’t cut it. Adoption requires ongoing, bite-sized, embedded training.Different people learn differently, some prefer video demos, some written guides, some live sessions. Offering multiple formats is essential.Make training contextual. Show how a tool helps that lawyer in that workflow and real life scenario. They don't have time to try and figure this out themselves.

#### 3. Mindset & Change Management

Resistance often isn’t about the tool. It’s fear: fear of losing control, of looking slow, reputational risk if it goes wrong, of change itself.Change needs champions. Identify early adopters in the legal team, support them, and let them become your advocates.Communicate successes. Celebrate small wins, share metrics, and spotlight those who embrace the tools. Adoption spreads socially as much as it does procedurally.

---

#### The Role of Use Cases

Here’s another trap I see too often: buying tech because it looks shiny on a demo.

Without a clear **use case** , adoption stalls.

Legal Ops should always ask:

What problem are we solving?Who benefits most?How do we measure success?

Because when you roll out, that becomes your story.

It's not: “This AI tool can auto-redline clauses.” but “This reduces your review time by 30% so you can leave the office at 7pm instead of midnight.”

The best adoption journeys are rooted in pain points lawyers actually feel day-to-day. If it doesn’t make their lives easier, they won’t use it. Simple as that.

---

#### Continuous Improvement: Adoption Never Stops

Here’s the truth nobody tells you: adoption isn’t a project. It’s a long-term commitment.

Processes evolve as teams grow.Business priorities change.Tech vendors update features constantly.

If Legal Ops doesn’t keep iterating, measuring, tweaking, and adapting, adoption will slide. The best teams treat technology like a living process, not a one-off implementation.

I’ve seen plenty of tools launch with a grand launch, only to be quietly abandoned a year or two later. Why? Because nobody “owned” them. No one was responsible for updating workflows, retraining the team, or making sure the tool kept pace with other systems the business actually used.

Now compare that with the teams that build in regular “health checks”, every quarter they review how tools and processes are working, what’s changed in the business, and where the friction points are. Those teams don’t treat improvement as a one-off project; it’s just part of how they operate. And the result is obvious: adoption stays strong, value keeps building, and the tech doesn’t turn into yet another piece of shelfware.

---

#### Why All This Matters for AI

And here’s where this links back to AI.

AI isn’t plug-and-play. It requires:

**Trust in the outputs.** **Governance around use.** **Processes that can absorb change.** **People who are open to using it.**

Without adoption, AI in legal will never deliver on its promise.

Legal Ops is the glue: the team that sets foundations, builds trust, manages governance, and ensures that technology, whether CLM, analytics, or AI, doesn’t just get bought but actually gets used.

Get this right, and you don’t just roll out tools. You build a legal function that’s ready for the future.
